## Why the Milky Way?

The Milky Way is arguably the most breathtaking subject in astrophotography. That arc of hundreds of billions of stars stretched across the sky connects us to something ancient and vast. Yet capturing it requires planning, patience, and a willingness to stay up very late.

## Choosing the Right Location

Light pollution is your biggest enemy. Use tools like **Light Pollution Map** or the **Clear Outside** app to find dark-sky sites near you. Even a short drive of 60–90 minutes from a city can make an enormous difference.

Key factors for a great location:

- Dark sky rating of **Bortle 4** or lower
- Unobstructed horizon toward the **galactic core** (south in the Northern Hemisphere)
- Interesting foreground — mountains, trees, rock formations, or water

## Timing Your Shoot

The galactic core is visible from **March to October** in the Northern Hemisphere. The very best months are **May through August** when the core rises high in the sky.

Always check:

1. **Moon phase** — shoot within 5 days of a new moon
2. **Core azimuth and altitude** — use the Photopills app to plan your exact composition
3. **Weather** — clear skies with low humidity are essential

## Camera Settings

| Setting | Starting point |
|---|---|
| Aperture | f/1.8 – f/2.8 |
| ISO | 3200 – 6400 |
| Shutter speed | 15 – 25 seconds (500 rule) |
| Focus | Manual, set to stars |
| Format | RAW |

The **500 rule**: divide 500 by your focal length to get the maximum shutter speed before stars start trailing. For a 24 mm lens: 500 ÷ 24 ≈ 20 seconds.

## Post-Processing Workflow

1. **White balance** — try around 3800K for a natural night feel
2. **Noise reduction** — apply luminance NR carefully to preserve star detail
3. **Contrast and clarity** — bring out the Milky Way structure
4. **Star mask** — create a luminosity mask to boost star brightness independently of the sky
5. **Foreground blend** — a separate foreground exposure at ISO 800 blended in post often gives cleaner results

## Final Thoughts

Astrophotography rewards patience more than gear. A kit lens at f/3.5 can still capture a beautiful Milky Way if you're in a truly dark location. Get out there, embrace the cold, and look up.
